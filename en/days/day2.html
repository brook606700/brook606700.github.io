Day 2｜Forests, Streams, and the Village of the Bridge

May 22, 2026 · Roncesvalles → Zubiri · 21km

The rules at the public albergue are ironclad: at ten at night, the front door locks sharp.

Last night, a young woman from Chengdu whom I had met on the trail just stepped into the courtyard to collect her laundry—and was mercilessly locked outside. Had I not still been awake past eleven and rescued her, she would probably have spent the night in the chilly ten-something-degree wind.

Why was I still awake at eleven? Because day one had truly wiped everyone out. The whole dorm was an epic multi-part snoring symphony—so powerful not even earplugs could block it.

After tossing through half the night in that symphony, feeling like I had only just closed my eyes, the 6am hymn wake-up call rang right on time. With a sigh, I reluctantly climbed down from the top bunk and was forced to greet a new day.

But yesterday's trial is behind us now.

Compared to the brutality of the Pyrenees on day one, today's route was as gentle as a hug. No endless steep slopes—just easy forest paths. About two kilometers out of town, I luckily found a little grocery shop by the roadside and bought a simple breakfast. The morning sun fell softly, and the moment I took a bite, I savored a simple, quiet moment.

Walking through woods dappled with fragmented sunlight, I suddenly felt that being woken by the hymn was not so bad after all. My tight legs were slowly waking up.

Passing a bar with a big platform scale outside, we got curious and took turns stepping on it. I wish we had not—my companions turned out to be absolute ironmen. One pack heavier than the next, the heaviest a full fifteen kilos. Carrying that kind of weight for nearly a thousand kilometers—I could only bow to them in silent respect.

Walking on, I heard water.

Following the sound of flowing water, the iconic medieval stone bridge appeared before me—I had reached today's destination, Zubiri. The name literally means "village of the bridge." The town has no church, no public albergue, but it has the crystal-clear Arga river, and ancient flagstone streets breathing quiet, simple age. A private albergue bed runs about fifteen euros.

Coming to this town, you have to get in the water. The stream was ice cold—the moment my feet went in, all of yesterday's fatigue seemed to flow away with the current. A little dog played with us by the stream, and watching the sparkling water, that moment of ease was truly priceless.

In the evening we bought groceries at the supermarket and squeezed into the little kitchen to cook dinner together. No head chef, no assistants—just a group of people chopping, boiling pasta, and drinking beer together. The meal cost almost nothing, yet tasted better than any restaurant. I discovered the Camino's best-kept secret about food—it is never the food itself, but the people you share it with.

Reading what Taiwanese pilgrims who stayed here days earlier had written:
"The world is vast, people are small. Grateful for every encounter and every helping hand, every smile. Thank you, Buen Camino."

The meaning of this road is not the destination, but every encounter along the way.
