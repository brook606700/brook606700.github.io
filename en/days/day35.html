Day 35｜The Full Stop After 1,000 km, the Bonfire Festival, Back to the Start

June 24, 2026 · Muxía → Santiago de Compostela · bus ride home

Once again I set out before sunrise! The destination is close — that 0-kilometer marker just 1.5 km away. Even though I'd heard this place had been overcast for a whole week, I still held onto a sliver of hope: to see the sunrise at the end of the world.

Walking along the coastline, Muxía's 0-kilometer marker appeared right before my eyes; the seaside church and the rocks from the final scene of the movie The Way came into view too — the whole picture felt almost unreal!

A crack split open in the heavy clouds, letting a pale morning light through. Full of anticipation, I climbed the small hill and looked down over the quiet coastal town, waiting for the first light of dawn. Just as I reached the top, the sunrise arrived — crepuscular rays piercing the clouds, warm morning light instantly illuminating the little houses by the shore and laying a golden road across the sea.

Standing beside the stone cross on the hill watching this sunrise, my heart was full of awe. The day before yesterday in Fisterra, we watched the sun sink into the sea and said goodbye to the past; and here in Muxía, overcast for a whole week, heaven gave us this sunrise — the symbol of a brand-new beginning.

On this road, you'll meet countless kinds of scenery — and countless kinds of life choices. The Chinese girl walking with us is a veteran hiker; she pushed through shin splints for days, but on this final day, she chose to take a ride to Muxía. Yet for a veteran, "persevering" is often the easy part — "letting go" is the hardest decision. Along this thousand-kilometer road there are countless stories of holding on and compromising. The Camino was never an exam chasing a perfect score — those regrets of unfinished miles, those moments of learning to surrender to your body, are often closer to the true meaning of this journey than a flawless finish.

This giant cracked-stone monument by the sea is called "The Wound". It records a devastating oil-tanker spill more than twenty years ago, and the story of countless volunteers working together to bring this Coast of Death back to life. No wonder people always say Fisterra means farewell, while Muxía means healing and rebirth. Looking at the crack in the middle of the boulder — only by walking through brokenness and pain can the light shine in.

Walking into the rocky shore area, the most special of all is this "sail stone" with a hole underneath! Legend says it's part of the stone boat of the Virgin Mary — crawl through the hole nine times and your ailments will be healed! But the opening is tiny, and it taught us at the finish line to "bow down" — passing through that stone hole is more like an ancient baptism: leaving heaviness and attachment behind on this shore, and taking a brand-new self with you.

Before my eyes stands the "Church of Our Lady of the Boat", standing guard by the waves and boulders for centuries over every traveler who reaches the end of the world. I opened my credencial, its pages full of stories, and stamped the final seal — the ultimate proof of over a thousand kilometers, one step at a time. In this moment, the most complete full stop.

Saying goodbye to the end of the world, we joined the line of pilgrims and boarded the bus back to Santiago. My feet had walked for days to get here; now just over an hour brings me effortlessly back to the start. The moment I stepped onto the bus, it truly sank in: the walking days are over, and we're heading back to the real world.

Back in Santiago, tonight's private albergue happens to sit right by the station. The owner here is incredibly warm — he not only treated me to stew but brought out a special wine to toast the celebration. I printed a Polaroid photo as a gift for him, and to my surprise he treasured it, hanging it up alongside banknotes from around the world — that human warmth was something special.

But the day wasn't over yet! I walked back to the square in front of Santiago Cathedral, soaking in the city's energy before nightfall. Walking along, someone suddenly called out to me — a Taiwanese follower from our group chat recognized me! He gave me a hand-drawn sticker full of Taiwanese flavor; meeting like this in a foreign land was so heartwarming. Fate brings people together, so I picked a popular online-famous restaurant to celebrate — and it turned out to be a total miss!

As night fell, tonight's main event was just beginning — the "Bonfire Festival" lighting up the whole city! This is Spain's famous Festival of San Juan: people welcome the summer solstice with bonfires, symbolizing burning away past misfortunes and purifying body and soul. Tradition says successfully jumping over a bonfire brings abundant good luck! We joined in with burning enthusiasm — except the reality was, legs that had just walked over a thousand kilometers felt like they were filled with lead, with no strength left at all! Watching the whole city grow wilder as the night went on, ending thirty-plus days of Camino with this lively, moving celebration — this memory is truly priceless!

📚 Pilgrim's mini-guide: The Festival of San Juan

The Night of San Juan (Noche de San Juan) is celebrated around the summer solstice each year (on the night of about June 23). It is one of Spain's most important festivals. People light bonfires on beaches and in squares, using firelight to drive away evil and welcome new life; tradition says jumping over the flames brings good luck. In Santiago, the old town parties all night — the most magical graduation ceremony for finishing the Camino.

🔥 If the timing works, definitely stay — this is a night that comes only once a year.

🦵 Before jumping the bonfire, take stock of your legs: after a thousand kilometers, they're barely legs anymore.
