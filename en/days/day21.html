Day 21｜The Iron Cross, Laying Down My Stone, and Knees That Aren't Mine

June 10, 2026 · Rabanal del Camino → Ponferrada · 31km

Day 21. I set off in the morning chill — barely 5 degrees. Three weeks in, this is a rare morning that actually feels cold. Before dawn, I climbed a scree slope in the dark, every step placed carefully, climbing toward the slowly rising sun.

Past that punishing scree slope, the morning fog still hung thick and sunlight peeked through the clouds onto the path. In that quiet, I walked on step by step along the light-and-shadow road; the fog scattered and gathered again, every turn feeling like a new scene.

Past a quiet wooden cross by the roadside, I pushed on toward the high point. Turning to look back, a sea of clouds was pouring silently over the distant peaks like a waterfall, brilliant sunlight spilling down — in that moment the whole world went quiet.

Looking up, a pure-white rainbow hung in the thick fog. Not my eyes playing tricks — it's the rare "fogbow": the droplets are too small to refract color, leaving only white. Meeting one in thick fog felt utterly magical; I stared for ages, unwilling to leave.

Finally — the highest point of the Camino Francés: the Iron Cross (Cruz de Ferro). Probably the quietest, most powerful scene I have ever seen, solemn and calm through the fog. For centuries, pilgrims have carried stones across mountains and rivers to lay them down here. Each stone holds someone's obsession, regret, or a burden they can't let go. I opened my hand and looked at my stone, inscribed "Buen Camino." Honestly, there was nothing I especially wanted to let go of — but as the stone settled gently onto the pile, leaving the past behind in the thick fog, an indescribable lightness settled over my heart.

Today's total: 31 km, 641 m of climbing — and a whopping 1,256 m of descent. The numbers may look fine, but only someone who has actually stood on that scree knows: sometimes going down is far harder than going up. My thoughts were still back at the Iron Cross — an unadorned wooden pole, yet beneath it an entire mountain of stones stacked by pilgrims over centuries, every stone hiding someone's secret, piled high with regrets, obsessions, even bad habits we should long have quit. We always think letting go means forgetting, but it doesn't. Letting go isn't denying what happened; it's admitting that the weight has been heavy enough, and you don't need to carry it as daily luggage anymore. We often dare not let go because we fear that without the burden we become hollow — but when you truly open your hand, you find you lost nothing. You simply finally freed your hands to embrace whatever life comes next. Turning away after laying down the stone, what faces me is still the brutal descent: the scree underfoot just as treacherous, my body growing no wings from one sacred ritual. Maybe that is life's most honest truth: letting go doesn't make problems vanish on the spot. The mountain is no lower, the road no flatter.

Trail-running mode: ON! Watch the footing, ride the momentum downhill, wind in my face — and I know something inside has genuinely loosened a little. This moment feels joyful. This non-stop steep descent is brutally hard: the cyclist ahead is picking his way down carefully while I charge right behind, landing each step with focus — that adrenaline rush is pure bliss. Knees can wait their turn.

Entering a tiny town of fewer than 20 people, the roadside hides this warm little donativo juice stand. Its sweetness was left for us by yesterday's pilgrims — and the coins we drop today will become the strength carrying another pilgrim forward tomorrow.

Finally off the mountain! Before entering town you pass this magnificent church, and by the town fountain a stone pillar covered in Japanese writing — a friendship monument from Japan's Shikoku pilgrimage. Meeting Japanese pilgrimage culture on the Spanish Camino — what a wonderful connection.

Finally at today's finish line: Ponferrada! Tonight I'm in this wonderfully bright municipal albergue, which even holds this milestone — only 210 km left to Santiago. Seeing that number gave me goosebumps all over.

This town holds the mighty Templar castle, straight out of a movie. In medieval times the Camino was desolate and full of bandits, and these elite knights with red crosses on their chests built fortresses and hospitals along the route to protect pilgrims — the original "pilgrim bodyguards." Centuries later the knights are gone, but their statues still quietly watch over us. Walking beneath the walls genuinely raises goosebumps.

Cooking for myself tonight: pan-fried chicken legs with red wine! A protein-packed finish to a perfect day — 31 km of fatigue melting away in that first bite of savory meat.

📚 Pilgrim's mini-guide: Cruz de Ferro

The Iron Cross (Cruz de Ferro) is the spiritual high point of the Camino Francés, on a ridge at about 1,500 meters. A humble wooden pole topped with a tiny iron cross — yet beneath it, a mountain of stones laid one by one by pilgrims over centuries.

The tradition: bring a stone from home. The stone stands for your burden, your regret, your longing — anything you want to properly say goodbye to. Lay it on the pile, leave that weight on the mountain, and walk on lightly.

One warning: after the Iron Cross comes a relentless steep scree descent — a knee-killer. Get the trekking poles out, shorten your stride, descend sideways, and don't pick a fight with your own knees.

🪨 Bring a stone from home — forgetting to will haunt you when you get there

🦵 Downhill hurts knees more than uphill; trekking poles + knee sleeves are standard kit
