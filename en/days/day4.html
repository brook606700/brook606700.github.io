Day 4｜Alto del Perdón, Stopped by Police, and Puente la Reina

May 24, 2026 · Pamplona → Puente la Reina · 24km

Setting off again before sunrise. Maybe because it was the weekend, the crowds from last night's revelry still lingered outside the bars at 5am. Shouldering my pack, I quietly brushed past this still-tipsy city.

Having walked Taiwan's Baishatun Mazu pilgrimage, I have to be honest: the endless ups and downs every single day here are definitely harder.

But today's fields were incredibly vast. An endless stretch of green filled my view, and the visual shock made me forget my tired feet. This kind of beauty can only truly be felt by walking it step by step. The vastness before me was a reminder of how small we are—yet how lucky to be free to feel this world.

Gasping my way up, I finally reached the summit. The row of iron pilgrims standing against the wind is an iconic landmark—Alto del Perdón. These life-size iron figures on the mountaintop represent pilgrims from different eras. The monument reads: "Here the path of the wind meets the path of the stars." Legend says that arriving here, you must learn to let go of the burdens in your heart—forgive others, and forgive yourself. Standing on the summit overlooking the vast land, I felt an indescribable calm and release.

Past Alto del Perdón came a boss-level steep gravel descent.

On downhills I used to press my weight back and brake hard, afraid of falling. But on a friend's tip, I switched straight into trail-running mode—going with the momentum, eyes locked on each foothold. Unexpectedly, not only did my downhill speed double, my knees felt liberated too.

Then the sun hit 40 degrees with zero shade. I was wrapped head to toe with a face covering against the sun, since my skin flares up with heat allergies and rashes. And then—I got stopped by the police, who demanded I remove the face covering. They probably took me for someone suspicious: all wrapped up in black, I looked rather like a terrorist. We all laughed, and after much explaining they let me go. Turns out Europe has anti-mask laws—another fun cultural difference to experience.

By noon I was melting in the heat, but luckily this bar appeared by the roadside—a classic rest stop featured in the film The Way. I ordered a giant chicken burger with an ice-cold beer for lunch. That first bite was truly the ultimate salvation on the Camino.

Spain's midday sun burns through even a parasol. Just as I was about to be roasted, a donation-based lemonade stand by the roadside became the ultimate rescue—one gulp and instant chill to the core. Next to it sat an adorable lemon-shaped stamp, a must for the pilgrim passport. On this road, it is often these unassuming little details that deliver the warmest surprises.

Passing a wedding in progress, I finally reached the town albergue. I quickly washed off the day's exhaustion, and took advantage of the blazing noon sun to dry my clothes—and my handmade soap. In barely ten minutes, Spain's vicious sun had melted a third of the soap away. The Camino is like that: even absurd little episodes become perfect stories.

In the afternoon I joined the church's pilgrim blessing. The priest placed a small metal cross on each of us. Tiny in size, light in weight—but the moment it hung on my chest, what it carried was not weight, but full blessings.

As the sun set, we arrived at Puente la Reina.

This 11th-century Romanesque stone bridge has its iconic six arches. Legend says a queen had it built specially to help pilgrims cross the river safely. A thousand years on, the flowing water and the stone bridge remain as they were. Standing on the bridge facing the sunset, the history of the Camino suddenly became tangible. The day's exhaustion dissolved completely in that thousand-year golden afterglow.

📚 Pilgrim's mini-guide: Alto del Perdón & Puente la Reina

Alto del Perdón is home to the Camino's most iconic image—the 1996 iron pilgrim sculptures by artist Vicente Galbete: a line of iron pilgrims and a donkey leaning into the wind. The inscription reads:

"Donde se cruza el camino del viento con el de las estrellas."

>

"Where the path of the wind meets the path of the stars."

Puente la Reina takes its name from that 11th-century Romanesque stone bridge, said to have been built by a queen (Doña Mayor) to help pilgrims cross the Arga river. Six arches, calmly spanning the river, unchanged for a millennium.

From here, the two pilgrim routes—the Aragonese Way from Somport and the French Way from Roncesvalles—officially merge into one, running together all the way to Santiago.

📸 Iron sculptures against blue sky and white clouds are the classic shot; the low afternoon light after 3pm is the most beautiful
